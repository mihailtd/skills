# Urgent versus Important Instructions

When supporting architecture design, use this tool to help teams apply the
change process with judgment rather than rigidly — recognizing genuinely
urgent work as an exception that shouldn't wait for full process, while
treating documentation and process discipline as advance preparation for
the next urgent situation, and steering effort away from work that is
neither urgent nor important.

---

## Purpose

This tool helps the AI assistant by:

- distinguishing truly urgent work (security incidents, legal deadlines,
  other exigent circumstances) from the ordinary flow of change, and
  permitting it to bypass full process rigor in the moment rather than
  insisting documentation and vision updates happen first,
- reframing rigorous system documentation as insurance rather than tax —
  a cost paid now, with uncertain timing of payoff, against the near
  certainty that an urgent situation will eventually arise,
- recognizing that most architecturally valuable work is important but not
  urgent, and that this is exactly the work most deserving of full process
  discipline — not a category to shortcut by default,
  and,
- explicitly permitting teams to drop work that is neither important nor
  urgent, rather than applying the full process ritualistically to
  everything that shows up,
- recommending calendar-protected, uninterrupted thinking time as the
  concrete mechanism for actually doing important-not-urgent work, since
  coordination and communication activities are structurally easy to see
  and schedule while focused thinking is not, despite being the core
  activity of the discipline.

---

## Expected outcome

As the AI, your response should help teams adopt an approach that:

- applies full change-proposal and documentation rigor as the default, but
  suspends it without guilt when work is genuinely, verifiably urgent,
- treats "we need this immediately" claims with judgment — testing whether
  the urgency is real (security, legal, other exigent circumstances) rather
  than accepting deadline pressure alone as justification for skipping
  process,
- maintains documentation discipline specifically *because* it pays off
  during future urgent situations, not despite the fact that most days it
  seems like unnecessary overhead,
- protects time for important-but-not-urgent work — most core architectural
  work — recognizing that this is precisely the work at risk of being
  perpetually deprioritized in favor of whatever feels urgent right now,
- is willing to recommend simply not doing work that is neither important
  nor urgent, rather than defaulting to running everything through the
  process,
- treats blocked, calendar-protected focus time as a legitimate default —
  not a luxury to justify — and recognizes that writing flows better once
  thinking has crystallized, so struggling to write is often a sign to step
  away and think rather than a sign to push through at the keyboard.

---

## Instructions for the AI

1. **Let genuinely urgent work bypass full process rigor**
   - When work is truly urgent — driven by security incidents, legal
     requirements, or other exigent, non-negotiable circumstances — don't
     insist that complete documentation, vision updates, or full
     change-proposal ceremony happen before acting.
   - Recommend teams do their best with what they have on hand in these
     situations, and treat that as the correct call, not a process failure.
   - Be honest about the tradeoff: skipping process for genuinely urgent
     work is the right call precisely because it's rare — this isn't
     license to treat routine deadline pressure the same way.

2. **Test whether claimed urgency is real**
   - When a team wants to skip process due to "urgency," probe whether the
     situation is genuinely exigent (security, legal, similarly
     non-negotiable) or whether it's ordinary deadline pressure being
     framed as urgent.
   - Ordinary feature deadlines are usually important, not urgent — treat
     that distinction as the deciding factor for whether process should be
     followed or set aside.

3. **Frame documentation as insurance, prepared in advance**
   - Make the case that the only good time to prepare for urgent work is
     before it arises — once an urgent situation hits, there's no time left
     to recover missing documentation.
   - Reframe documentation maintenance as insurance rather than tax: it has
     a real, ongoing cost and uncertain timing of payoff, but the
     probability that it eventually pays off (some disaster striking) is
     high over a long enough horizon.
   - Use this framing specifically when a team is tempted to deprioritize
     documentation discipline during calm periods — the value shows up
     exactly when things aren't calm.

4. **Protect important-but-not-urgent work as the priority, not the shortcut**
   - Point out that most valuable architectural work — accurate system
     documentation, disciplined change proposals, thoughtful alternative
     generation — is important but rarely urgent, and is therefore
     structurally at risk of being crowded out by whatever feels urgent in
     the moment (even when that urgency is only apparent, per step 2).
   - Recommend treating this important-not-urgent work as deserving the
     most careful, thorough handling specifically because nothing else is
     forcing time to be spent on it — it has to be protected deliberately.

5. **Recommend dropping work that's neither important nor urgent**
   - When work is genuinely neither important nor urgent, recommend not
     doing it at all, rather than running it through the full process by
     default — process discipline is for protecting effort spent on things
     that matter, not for legitimizing effort spent on things that don't.
   - Offer a concrete test for "unimportant": ask what would happen if the
     item were simply ignored. If the honest answer is "nothing bad," treat
     that as sufficient grounds to drop it, even if it's fun, interesting,
     or otherwise appealing to engage with.

6. **Protect calendar time for focused thinking specifically**
   - Point out the structural bias at work: coordination (meetings,
     scheduling) and communication (documents, reviews) are easy to see and
     therefore easy to schedule for, while thinking — the activity actually
     at the core of the discipline — is easy to overlook precisely because
     it doesn't visibly demand calendar space the way a meeting does.
   - Recommend blocking recurring, protected "focus time" on the calendar
     explicitly, the same way meeting time gets blocked — and treat this as
     a default practice worth encouraging (or requiring) across the team,
     not an individual indulgence that needs special justification.
   - Recommend against jumping straight to writing before thinking has
     crystallized: writing flows more easily once the thinking behind it is
     settled, and forcing the two to happen simultaneously tends to slow
     both down. When writing isn't flowing, the better move is usually to
     step away from the keyboard and think, not to push through.
   - Frame important-but-not-urgent work concretely as preparing in
     advance — building a plan and setting it aside for when it's needed —
     and connect this directly to architecture's specific mandate: since
     architecture is fundamentally about principles governing a system's
     evolution, thinking ahead of what will eventually become urgent is not
     a nice-to-have, it's close to the job's core definition.

## AI decision guidance

When generating urgent-vs-important guidance, keep these principles in
mind:

- **Urgency is an exception, verified case by case:** don't let deadline
  pressure alone substitute for a real urgency test (security, legal,
  similarly exigent).
- **Insurance framing, not tax framing:** always connect documentation
  discipline back to the future urgent situation it's preparing the team
  for, especially when someone is questioning its value.
- **Important-not-urgent is the default priority, not the fallback:**
  actively protect time for this category rather than letting it be
  perpetually deferred.
- **"Don't do it" is a legitimate recommendation:** for work that's neither
  important nor urgent, recommend dropping it rather than processing it.
- **Thinking time needs the same calendar protection as meetings:** don't
  let it stay invisible just because it's harder to schedule around than
  coordination work.
- **Struggling to write usually means more thinking is needed, not more
  typing:** redirect toward stepping away and thinking rather than pushing
  through a stuck draft.

---

## Success criteria

A strong urgent-vs-important response should help teams achieve:

- **judicious process bypass:** full process rigor suspended only for
  verified, genuinely exigent situations, not routine deadline pressure,
- **maintained documentation discipline:** system documentation kept
  current specifically because of the insurance value it provides during
  future urgent situations,
- **protected important work:** important-but-not-urgent architectural work
  visibly prioritized rather than continuously crowded out,
- **pruned low-value work:** work that's neither important nor urgent
  explicitly identified and dropped rather than processed by default,
- **protected thinking time:** recurring, calendar-blocked focus time
  treated as a default practice, with writing deliberately sequenced after
  thinking rather than forced to happen simultaneously.

  current?"

  work — how do I protect time for the important-but-not-urgent stuff?"

  through a design — how should I fix that?"

  should I do?"
